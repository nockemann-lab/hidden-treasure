1. Overview

Every smartphone contains dozens of chemical elements, many of which are classed as critical raw materials: they are essential for modern technology, but their supply is at risk. This resource lets learners take a phone apart virtually, find out which elements are inside each part, what they do, where they come from, and why recycling matters.

Age rangeDesigned for ages 11–16. Works with younger pupils with support, and as a discussion starter for post-16 and adult audiences.
Time15 minutes (explore + quiz) up to a full 50–60 minute lesson.
EquipmentAny device with a web browser: computer, tablet, phone or interactive whiteboard. Touch-friendly. Nothing to install.
SubjectsChemistry / science, geography, sustainability and citizenship, design & technology.
ContentsInteractive phone: six parts (display, camera, circuit board, speaker & magnets, battery, casing) with 20 elements and materials, each with its role, facts, origin and critical-list status.
Quiz: 10 random questions from a bank of 22, with instant feedback, explanations and a score.
Sources: all data is referenced on the main page.

How it works

  1. Select a phone part by tapping it in the exploded view of the phone.
  2. Tap an element tile to see a photo of the element and read what it does, a fun fact and where it comes from.
  3. A red corner on a tile (and a red info box) means the element is on the EU or US critical-materials list.
  4. Each part also shows statistics from a scientific study of old mobile phones, e.g. the value of the metals per tonne.
  5. When pupils feel ready, they take the quiz.

2. Learning objectives

By the end of the activity, learners should be able to:

  • name the main parts of a smartphone and some of the elements each part contains;
  • link the properties of an element to its use (e.g. gold does not corrode, copper conducts electricity, neodymium makes strong magnets, lithium is very light);
  • explain what makes a raw material "critical" and give examples of supply being concentrated in a few countries;
  • describe environmental, social and ethical issues linked to mining (e.g. cobalt mining, conflict minerals);
  • explain why old phones are a valuable "urban mine" and why reuse, repair and recycling matter;
  • (extension) interpret and compare data, e.g. metal concentrations in phones versus ores.

3. Background for teachers

What is a "critical" raw material?

Governments publish lists of materials that are both important to the economy and have a supply that is at risk, for example because production is concentrated in one or a few countries, or because they are hard to substitute or recycle.

  • EU: the Critical Raw Materials Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1252) lists 34 critical raw materials, 17 of which are also "strategic" (key for green and digital technologies, defence and space).
  • USA: the US Geological Survey's 2025 list contains 60 critical minerals, defined as essential to economic or national security and with a supply chain vulnerable to disruption.

The lists are reviewed every few years, so an element's status can change. For example, indium was on the EU's 2020 list but not the 2023 list, and copper and silver were added to the US list in 2025.

Key messages

  • Small amounts, big value: gold, silver, platinum and palladium make up less than 1.5% of the metal in a phone, but 93% of its metal value. Copper is more than half of the metal by weight.
  • Urban mining: old phones are richer in many metals than natural ores. A tonne of phone circuit boards can contain hundreds of times more gold than a tonne of gold ore.
  • Concentrated supply: e.g. DR Congo mined 73% of the world's cobalt in 2025; China produced 99% of primary gallium and mined about 69% of rare earths.
  • Ethics: tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold are "conflict minerals" (3TG) – companies in the US and EU must check their supply chains don't fund armed groups. Some cobalt is mined by hand in dangerous conditions, sometimes by children.
  • Recycling gap: in 2022 only about 22% of the world's e-waste was documented as properly collected and recycled.

5. Suggested lesson plan (50 minutes)

TimeActivity
5 minStarter: "How many different elements do you think are in your phone?" Collect guesses. (Answer: 60–70 – most of the stable elements in the periodic table!)
20 minExplore: in pairs, pupils explore all six phone parts and fill in the student worksheet – one element per part: what it does, a fact, and whether it is critical. Alternatively, give each pair one part and have them report back.
10 minQuiz: pupils take the quiz individually or in pairs and note their score. They can play again for a new set of questions.
10 minDiscussion: choose two or three of the discussion questions.
5 minPlenary: "What will you do with your next old phone?" Each pupil writes one action.
💡 Short on time? Use it as a 15-minute starter or homework: explore for 10 minutes, then take the quiz.

6. Discussion questions

Why might a pile of old phones be called an "urban mine"?

Old phones contain far higher concentrations of some metals than natural ores – e.g. a tonne of phone circuit boards can contain up to 678 g of gold, against 1–3 g in a tonne of typical gold ore. Recovering them can reduce the need for new mining.

Copper is more than half of the metal in a phone, but gold is about three-quarters of its value. Why?

Value depends on both the amount and the price. Gold is several thousand times more expensive than copper per kilogram, so even tiny amounts add up. This also explains why recyclers focus on circuit boards and cameras.

What could happen if one country produces almost all of an element?

Prices can rise sharply, and supply can be interrupted by export restrictions, conflict, natural disasters or trade disputes. Countries respond by diversifying supply, stockpiling, finding substitutes and recycling more.

Who should be responsible for recycling phones – users, manufacturers or governments?

There is no single right answer. Points to raise: take-back schemes and "producer responsibility" rules; deposit schemes; making collection easy; designing phones that are easier to take apart.

Should phones be easier to repair?

Repair keeps phones in use for longer, so fewer new materials need to be mined. In the EU, new rules (the Batteries Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2023/1542) will require batteries in many portable devices to be removable and replaceable by users from 2027.

Is it fair that some of the materials in our phones are mined in dangerous conditions?

Discuss artisanal cobalt mining and conflict minerals. What can consumers, companies and governments do? (Supply-chain checks, certification, buying second-hand, keeping phones longer.)

7. Extension activities

Data task: how many phones for 1 g of gold?

Old mobile phones can contain up to 141 g of gold per tonne (Gómez et al., 2023). A phone weighs about 100 g. How many old phones would contain 1 g of gold?

Show worked answer

1 tonne = 1,000,000 g, so 1 tonne ≈ 10,000 phones of 100 g each.
141 g of gold ÷ 10,000 phones ≈ 0.014 g of gold per phone.
1 g ÷ 0.014 g ≈ about 70 phones (at best – many phones contain less).

Data task: richer than ore

Phone circuit boards contain about 28% copper. Copper ore contains about 0.5–3% copper. How many times richer in copper are circuit boards?

Show worked answer

28 ÷ 3 ≈ 9 and 28 ÷ 0.5 = 56, so circuit boards are roughly 10 to 50 times richer in copper than copper ore.

More ideas

  • Periodic table hunt: colour in all the elements from the resource on a blank periodic table. Where are they? What do the rare earths and the platinum-group metals have in common?
  • Supply-chain research: pick one critical element and create a poster: where it is mined, how it is refined, what it is used for, and the risks.
  • Drawer phones survey: how many unused phones do pupils' households have? Estimate the gold in all of them using the data task above.
  • Design challenge: design a phone that is easy to repair and recycle.
⚠️ Safety: do not take real phones or lithium-ion batteries apart in class. Damaged batteries can catch fire. Never put phones or batteries in the household bin.

8. Using it at events and exhibitions

  • Open index.html in a web browser and switch to full screen (F11 on Windows, Ctrl + Cmd + F on Mac).
  • It works well on a touchscreen or tablet. Reload the page to reset it for the next visitor.
  • All files can be downloaded and run without an internet connection (only the links to external sources need one).
  • Place a second device next to it running the quiz, so visitors can explore first and then test themselves.

9. About the data

  • The component values, weights and metal contents come from a peer-reviewed study of 15 old mobile phones made between 2002 and 2015 (Gómez et al., 2023). Modern phones may differ – e.g. most new phones now use OLED rather than LCD screens.
  • Values are the gross value of the metals contained, at 2021 prices. The real value recovered is lower, because collection, dismantling and refining cost money and not everything can be recovered.
  • Batteries were not analysed in that study; battery facts come from other sources.
  • Production shares (e.g. "China produced 99% of primary gallium") are for 2025, from the US Geological Survey's Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026.
  • Critical-list status uses the EU list (2023/2024) and the US list (2025).
